Frequently Asked Questions

Plain answers to the questions we hear most, about what a muni utility is, how it would work, and what this campaign is actually asking for.

The Basics

A municipal electric utility (or "muni") is an electric utility owned and operated by a city government on behalf of its residents. Instead of a private corporation with shareholders, the utility is governed locally, either directly by the city council or by an appointed utility board made up of community members. Revenue goes back into the utility and the community, not to distant investors.
Nearly 2,000 cities across the United States do it right now. In Iowa alone, Cedar Falls, Ames, and Muscatine have run their own electric utilities for over a century. These aren't small towns, Cedar Falls and Ames are comparable in size and complexity to Cedar Rapids neighborhoods. Municipal utilities hire professional staff, engineers, and managers just like any other utility. The city doesn't need to figure everything out from scratch; there are established models, professional associations (like the American Public Power Association), and neighboring cities to learn from.
Alliant Energy is a publicly traded company (NASDAQ: LNT). By law, it must generate returns for its shareholders. That profit margin is built into your electric rate. A municipal utility has no shareholders, every dollar collected goes toward operating costs, infrastructure, and reserves. Municipal utilities also generally pay lower borrowing costs (municipal bond rates vs. corporate debt), which matters when financing large infrastructure. The combination of no profit obligation and cheaper financing is why Iowa munis consistently charge less per kilowatt-hour than Alliant.
Yes, essentially. Municipalization means the city acquires the electric distribution infrastructure, the wires, transformers, substations, and equipment, that currently delivers power to Cedar Rapids homes and businesses. Alliant owns that infrastructure right now. The city would negotiate to purchase it, or in some cases pursue condemnation (eminent domain) if negotiation fails. The city would then operate it as a public utility. Generation (where the electricity actually comes from) is a separate question, many munis buy wholesale power from regional markets rather than owning their own power plants, at least initially.

The Process & Timeline

Right now, we are asking for one thing: the right to vote on it. We are not asking the city to acquire Alliant's infrastructure unilaterally. We are building the community support and evidence base needed to place a municipal utility referendum on the 2027 Cedar Rapids ballot. If voters approve the referendum, it gives the city a mandate to pursue acquisition through the proper legal and regulatory channels. The voters of Cedar Rapids decide, not the campaign, not the city council alone.
November 2027 is the next Cedar Rapids city election. Under Iowa law, a municipal utility referendum must go on the regular city ballot. That is the nearest opportunity we have to put this question to voters. We need to use the time between now and then to build the petition, compile the data, and make the case so voters can make an informed decision.
Iowa Code Chapter 388 governs municipal utilities. A city can establish a municipal utility, but acquiring an existing investor-owned utility's service territory involves the Iowa Utilities Board (IUB). The IUB must find that the transfer is in the public interest. If the city and Alliant cannot agree on a purchase price, eminent domain proceedings are possible but complex. We are actively researching the exact legal pathway and will publish our findings on the Data page as we go.
Almost certainly. Alliant Energy would likely spend significant resources opposing a Cedar Rapids municipalization effort, lobbying the city council, funding opposition campaigns, and challenging the process at the IUB. This is why we need an overwhelming community mandate and a rigorous, well-documented case. The stronger our data and the broader our coalition, the harder it is for Alliant to argue that this is fringe opposition rather than mainstream Cedar Rapids public opinion.

There is a deep irony here. Every dollar Alliant spends fighting this effort will be passed back to ratepayers through their rates. The very customers asking for fair rates will end up paying for Alliant's campaign to keep charging them too much. All we are asking for is a fair shot at fair rates.

Concerns & Tradeoffs

Yes, acquisition would be a significant capital investment. Cities typically finance it through municipal revenue bonds, which are repaid from utility revenues (not general tax dollars). If the acquired utility generates lower operating costs and stable revenue, the bonds are self-financing over time. The key question is whether the long-term savings to Cedar Rapids ratepayers outweigh the acquisition and financing costs. That analysis, a proper acquisition feasibility study, is one of the most important things this campaign needs to produce. We won't ask voters to approve something we haven't modeled financially.
It's a fair concern. The answer is accountability. A municipal utility's governing board is directly accountable to Cedar Rapids voters, unlike Alliant's board, which is accountable to shareholders in 49 states. If a muni is mismanaged, residents can vote out the council members responsible, attend public board meetings, and demand change. With Alliant, your recourse is filing a complaint with the IUB and hoping for the best. Local accountability is an imperfect protection, but it's meaningfully better than none.
Cedar Rapids is Alliant's single largest load center in Iowa. Removing Cedar Rapids from Alliant's Iowa rate base would affect how costs are distributed across remaining Iowa customers. This is a legitimate concern and a factor the Iowa Utilities Board would examine. It is not a reason to avoid the question, but it is something that would need to be analyzed carefully and addressed in the IUB proceeding. Any transition would be designed to avoid unfairly burdening other Iowa ratepayers.
